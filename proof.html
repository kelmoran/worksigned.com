Trust & Verification

How the proof works

WorkSigned signs a daily record of a researcher’s work and pins it to a public, hash-chained log that an independent time-stamp issuer signs once a day.

The idea: Each day, a hash, or digital fingerprint, is generated from the work. WorkSigned signs that hash with the day’s date and the researcher’s identity, links it to the previous day’s hash, and writes it to a public, hash-chained log. One independent time-stamp issuer signs our log, once per day. The researcher holds the signed record + the actual work files as their proof.

The layers

The proof is built in layers, each one adding a specific guarantee. Understanding which layer does the work is the whole point.

LayerWhat it isWhat it proves
Original data The day’s files, file events, user events, and optional biometrics — the actual work. This is the work itself. It remains with the researcher and the institution.
Snapshot archive A packaged copy of the original data. Required with the signed record. Without it, a verifier holds a hash and nothing behind it.
Snapshot hash + size One hash of the archive, and its size. On its own: nothing. Both values can be changed at will. The signature below is what makes it meaningful.
Signed record The snapshot hash + size, the day’s date, the researcher’s identity, and a link to the prior record — signed with WorkSigned’s private key. The researcher held this exact hash on that date, in this order. The archive re-hashes to it; change one byte and it no longer verifies.
Signed record + archive The signed record together with the actual work files. The researcher held this work on that date, and the archive is the work the record points to. The complete, checkable proof.

What about AI?

WorkSigned does not attest that the work was unaided. But it records more than the finished file. It records the keystrokes, the optional biometrics, and the day-to-day evolution of the work as it is produced.

That leaves a trail a reviewer can read. Work that is pasted in all at once looks very different from work that is built up over hours and days, with edits, corrections, and drafts. The record makes that difference visible.

So the system gives strong, convincing evidence of how the work was made. It is not a definitive proof of human authorship. But it is the kind of record that can provide strong evidence of a human vs. AI origin of work. AI typing does exist, but to date no known system exists that can recreate the entire work process action by action, including the intermediate steps, in a human-like way.

In a dispute

Stated plainly. WorkSigned settles the important question that matters between claimants: who held the work first. It does not claim the work was original or unaided — credit is a human claim, and the system proves priority: that a specific researcher committed a specific body of work to a verifiable, independently time-stamped record on a specific date, in an unbroken chain.

It also shows the path the data took. The record captures the external services and systems the work contacted during production. In a dispute over access to sensitive work, this gives a dated, verifiable account of what the data touched, when, and how it changed.

Why not just time-stamp my own work?

A researcher can, and often does, hash their own work and send it to a time-stamp authority directly. That produces a valid time-stamp: it proves a specific piece of data existed on a specific date. It is a reasonable way to protect a single file.

It does not, on its own, produce the same kind of record. A self-issued time-stamp is a single point in time. It is not a chain. It is not bound to a verified identity. And, most importantly in a dispute, it is a record that only the claimant holds and only the claimant produces. Its internal math can be verified, but its origin cannot — the other party can always argue it was assembled, and time-stamped, at the claimant’s own convenience, at the time of the dispute.

The WorkSigned record differs in one specific way: it is not produced only by the claimant. It is a record in a public, append-only log, anchored by a time-stamp issuer the claimant does not control. The record was written to that log before any dispute existed, and it is readable by any party without the claimant’s cooperation. That is the difference between a record a party can verify on its own and a record that is independently attested — the kind that carries weight when the party who made it is also the party who stands to win.

The public log

The log is the keystone. It is public, append-only, hash-chained, and readable by any party.

Once a day, an independent time-stamp issuer stamps the log’s head to pin the entire chain below it to a neutral clock. This gives credibility to the timestamps we issue.

Standards

The record is not a proprietary format. The snapshot hash is a standard SHA-256 digest, the signature is a standard public-key signature verifiable with the published key, and the daily time-stamp is issued by an independent time-stamp issuer — so a verifier checks the work against open standards rather than trusting WorkSigned. The signing is open and the logs are append-only and public: any party can confirm exactly what produced a given record, and the private key never leaves the authority. It is also light: only truncated hashes and metadata are stored per record — a few hundred bytes — never the researcher’s files.
